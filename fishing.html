The kit

Six things in the tackle box

A spinning combo is the easiest setup to learn. You can buy everything below for about the price of a nice dinner, and none of it needs upgrading for the first season.

Rod and reel6'6" medium-light spinning combo

Long enough to cast a light bobber, soft enough to feel a bluegill bite.

Line6 lb monofilament

Cheap, forgiving, and easy to tie. Respool once a season.

Hooks#6 to #10 baitholder

Small hooks catch big fish too. Large hooks only catch big fish.

BaitNightcrawlers

Pinch off a 1" piece. Almost every freshwater fish will eat it.

Hook sizes

The numbers run backwards

Plain sizes shrink as the number grows, so a #10 is smaller than a #2. After #1 the scale flips to "aught" sizes, where 1/0, 2/0 and 3/0 get bigger. The red rings are the beginner range.

Knots

Three knots cover almost everything

Wet the line with a little water or spit before you pull a knot tight. Dry line heats up from the friction and weakens.

Improved clinch

Tie a hook or lure to mono. Learn this one first.

  1. Pass the line through the hook eye.
  2. Wrap the tag end around the main line 5–7 times.
  3. Thread the tag back through the small loop by the eye, then through the big loop you just made.
  4. Wet it, pull tight, trim the tag.

Palomar

The strongest simple knot, good for braid.

  1. Double 6" of line and push the loop through the eye.
  2. Tie a loose overhand knot with the doubled line.
  3. Pass the hook through the loop.
  4. Wet it, pull both strands tight, trim.

Double uni

Join two lines, such as new line to old backing.

  1. Overlap the two line ends by about 8".
  2. With one end, tie a uni knot of 4–6 wraps around the other line.
  3. Repeat with the second end in the other direction.
  4. Pull the main lines apart until the knots slide together.

On the bank

Before you cast

  • Buy a license. Most US states require one from age 16, sold online by the state wildlife agency. Many states run a few free-fishing days each year.
  • Check the limits. Size and bag limits change by lake and species. The regulations booklet lists them.
  • Wet your hands before you touch a fish you plan to release. Dry hands strip the slime coat that protects it from infection.
  • Keep released fish in the water while you unhook them, and use pliers on a swallowed hook. Cutting the line close is kinder than digging it out.
  • Pack out your line. Discarded monofilament tangles birds and turtles for years.
Rules vary by state and water body. Check your local fishing regulations before you go.
